\section{Broadcast Algorithms for MANETs}
\label{sec:background}

A mobile ad-hoc network can be seen as a graph $G = (V, E)$, where $V$ is the set of
nodes, and $E$ is the set of links connecting nodes in the communication range.
In such networks, the broadcast problem consists in delivering a message to all nodes by only using links in $E$~\cite{Ruiz2015}.
Unfortunately, the simplest solution, known as \textit{flooding}, has a major drawback -- the \textit{broadcast storm problem}~\cite{Ni:1999:BSP:313451.313525}.
Indeed, since in flooding each node retransmits every received message, the medium is rapidly saturated.
This increases the error-rate in the network, preventing the correct dissemination of the message, affecting the communication and wasting resources.

In general, there are two issues to address in designing a broadcast algorithm for MANETs.
The first issue is guaranteeing a good level of network \textit{coverage} (percentage of nodes that receive a broadcast message).
Doing so in the context of MANETs is challenging because they may suffer from unique problems such as low throughput, temporary disconnection, high mobility, high bit error rate and varying characteristics over short timescales.
The second issue is minimizing the amount of resources used to perform the broadcasting.
For instance, reducing the number of retransmissions to avoid the congestion of the medium is considered a good practice.
Likewise, since the devices connected to a MANET tend to be running on batteries, it is worthwhile keeping power consumption as low as possible.

As shown in a thoughtful survey by Ruiz et al.~\cite{Ruiz2015}, the literature on broadcasting algorithms for MANETs is quite large \inti{I don't like large}.
In the current state of the art, we find similar approaches, but we also find mechanisms that greatly differ.
Although there are different classifications~\cite{Williams:2002:CBT:513800.513825, WuLou1043098, YiGerla2003}, we prefer the taxonomy presented in the aforementioned survey because it is newer and cover more approaches.  


In this paper we focus on broadcasting algorithms for mobile ad-hoc networks.
To ease the discussion about the algorithms that are evaluated hereafter, we rely on the taxonomy proposed  by Ruiz et al.~\cite{Ruiz2015}.
In such survey, the authors present many broadcasting algorithms and propose a taxonomy of features to classify them.
In addition, the survey summarizes the terminology used in the literature.
We use the following subset of those terms in the remainder of this paper:

\newtheorem{mydef}{Definition}


\begin{mydef}[Broadcast Session]
	In an ad-hoc network, nodes $V^\prime \subseteq V$ may aim at disseminating the messages $m_0, m_1, \dots, m_k$.
	The dissemination of a message $m_i$ is a \textit{broadcast session}; and it can be uniquely identified by the message.
\end{mydef}

\begin{mydef}[Underlying Topology]
	Dissemination algorithms often maintain a subset of nodes -- the underlying topology -- used to drive the broadcasting process. Those nodes, which play a special role during a broadcast session, are determined using strategies specifics to each dissemination technique.
\end{mydef}
