\section{Broadcast Algorithms for MANETs}
\label{sec:background}

A mobile ad-hoc network can be seen as a graph $G = (V, E)$, where $V$ is the set of
nodes, and $E$ is the set of links connecting nodes in the communication range.
In such networks, the broadcast problem consists in delivering a message to all nodes by only using links in $E$~\cite{Ruiz2015}.
Unfortunately, the simplest solution, known as \textit{flooding}, has a major drawback -- the \textit{broadcast storm problem}~\cite{Ni:1999:BSP:313451.313525}.
Indeed, since in flooding each node retransmits every received message, the medium is rapidly saturated.
This increases the error-rate in the network, preventing the correct dissemination of the message, affecting the communication and wasting resources.

In general, there are two issues to address in designing a broadcast algorithm for MANETs.
The first issue is guaranteeing a good level of network \textit{coverage} (percentage of nodes that receive a broadcast message).
Dealing with this issue in the context of MANETs is challenging because they may suffer from unique problems such as low throughput, temporary disconnection, high mobility, high bit error rate and varying characteristics over short timescales.
The second issue is minimizing the amount of resources used to perform the broadcasting.
For instance, reducing the number of retransmissions to avoid the congestion of the medium is considered a good practice.
Likewise, since the devices connected to a MANET tend to be running on batteries, it is worthwhile keeping energy consumption as low as possible.

As shown in a thoughtful survey by Ruiz et al.~\cite{Ruiz2015}, the literature on broadcasting algorithms for MANETs is quite large \inti{I don't like large}.
For that reason, we find a great diversity of approaches in the current state-of-the-art. 
To classify them, different criteria have been used~\cite{Williams:2002:CBT:513800.513825, WuLou1043098, YiGerla2003}.
However, in this paper we prefer the taxonomy presented by Ruiz et al. in the aforementioned survey because it is newer, more systematic and covers more approaches.
In the rest of this section we briefly discuss such a taxonomy.

First, some algorithms build an \textit{underlying topology} and use it to drive the broadcasting process. This \textit{topology} simply is a subset of nodes with some characteristics that depend on the algorithm.
Using an underlying topology  aim at reducing both network congestion and energy consumption.
However, building an underlying topology has a cost that can only be amortized if it is used in many \textit{broadcast sessions}.
