With the rise of what we call the Internet of Things (IoT), both the number and the diversity of devices that become connected is staggering. We are entering in a new era where all objects of our daily life, for instance, from mobile to wearable devices can be interconnected. Industrials, such as Cisco, predict that there will be 25 billion devices connected together by 2015 and 50 billion by 2020. The IoT aim is not really to enable users to interact with everything connected. It is more about crosslinking objects to promote machine-to-machine interactions, enabling so to massively collect and/or distribute data to be turned into information delivered to end-users to improve their interactions with their social and/or environmental context. 
IoT leads to a strong revival of interest in how to disseminate and/or collect data in mobile \textit{ad hoc} networks (MANET). In such networks, nodes are moving around, randomly appearing or disappearing, causing frequent changes in the network topology, and thus frequent and temporary disconnections. 

Over the last decade, hundreds of algorithms dedicated to such networks have emerged to either control, adapt or maintain network topology, making MANET networks a key enabler for connecting anything at anytime. However, determining which algorithm to leverage on to build an IoT application that provides the best trade of among low latency, low hop count distance, low energy consumption, with fault tolerant properties, seems difficult or even impossible or counterproductive. Most of the related network functions in a MANET network, such as routing for instance, rely on the fundamental broadcasting operation. 
Unfortunately, the simplest broadcast, also known as \textit{blind flooding}, to send a message from one node to all other nodes of the network suffers from a major drawback -- the \textit{broadcast storm problem}~\cite{Ni:1999:BSP:313451.313525}. Since nodes interact with each other \textit{via} radio communication, and each node retransmits every received message, the medium becomes rapidly saturated. In fact, when a single message is broadcasted, several hosts are covered according to the sender's transmission range. This implicitly increases the error-rate in the network. It has several consequences such as preventing the correct dissemination of messages, affecting the communication and wasting resources. 

Accordingly, designing a broadcast algorithm for MANETs is a trade of between two aims: (i) guaranteeing a suitable level of network coverage to optimize the percentage of nodes that receive a message, (ii) minimizing the amount of resources used when a broadcast operation is achieved. The underlaying hypothesis is that it is not required for a node to retransmit a received message from a given neighbor to other neighbors if they are already covered by the sender’s transmission range. As a consequence, reducing the number of retransmissions enables to avoid energy depletion of nodes than run on batteries, and thus to improve the overall network life time. 

However,  determining the minimum energy broadcast tree is NP-Hard. 
Hence, many approximate solutions have emerged based on various heuristics. Some algorithms adjust dynamically the transmission range $\textit{Tx}$ during the broadcasting process to either improve the coverage and/or reduce resource usage.
