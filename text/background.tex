\section{Motivations}
\label{sec:background}

With the rise of what we call the Internet of Things (IoT), both the number and the diversity of connected devices is staggering.
We are entering in a new era where all objects of our daily life, from mobile to wearable devices, can be interconnected.
Leaders of the industry, such as Cisco, predict that there will be 25 billion devices connected together by 2015 and 50 billion by 2020.
The goal of IoT is not really to enable users to interact with everything.
Instead, it is more about cross-linking objects to promote machine-to-machine interactions.
These interactions enable the massive capture and/or distribution of data that can be turned into information and delivered to end-users to improve their interaction with their social and environmental context.

IoT has led to a strong revival of interest in how to disseminate and collect data in mobile ad-hoc networks (MANET).
In such a network, nodes are moving around, randomly appearing or disappearing, causing frequent changes in the network topology, and thus leading to temporary disconnections.
Over the last decade, many algorithms have emerged to either control, adapt or maintain the topologies of such networks.
These advances have made MANET a key enabler for connecting anything at anytime.
However, determining which algorithm to leverage on to build an IoT application that provides the best trade-off among low latency, low energy consumption, and fault tolerant properties, remains a challenging endeavour.

Most of network functions in MANETs, such as routing for instance, rely on the fundamental \textit{broadcasting operation} which consists in delivering a message from a source node to all other nodes in a network.
A straight-forward approach to broadcast is by \textit{flooding}.
A host, on receiving a broadcast message for the first time, has the obligation to rebroadcast the message to its neighbors. 
Although simple, this approach suffers from a number of  drawbacks known as the \textit{broadcast storm problem}~\cite{Ni:1999:BSP:313451.313525}.
Because each node retransmits every received message, the number of redundant rebroadcasts becomes high. In addition, since they they do so \textit{via} radio waves and the transmission range of different nodes tend to overlap, the communication medium becomes rapidly saturated. Collisions are more likely to occur and cause more damage. In other words, both network bandwidth and energy are wasted. 



Because of the broadcast storm problem, designing a broadcasting algorithm for MANETs is a trade-off between two goals: (i) guaranteeing a suitable level of network coverage, which is the percentage of nodes that receive a message, and (ii) minimizing the amount of resources used when a broadcast operation is performed.
As a consequence, broadcasting algorithms attempt to reduce the number of nodes retransmitting a message to the minimum required to reach an acceptable coverage.
This avoids energy depletion of nodes than run on batteries, and thus improves the overall network life time.
In practice, however, it is hard to decide which nodes should retransmit a message.


For instance, the lack of a central authority in MANETs implies that the algorithms must be fully distributed and rely only on \textit{local} data available on each node.
In addition, they must take into account a variety of parameters such as the mobility patterns of the nodes and their heterogeneous characteristics (type of antenna, battery capacity \dots).
Many different techniques have been proposed for efficient message dissemination based on local data along with some heuristics.

\paragraph{topology-based algorithms}

A large family is composed by algorithms that build and use an \textit{underlaying topology} (or backbone) to drive the broadcast operation.
This underlaying topology is simply a subset of nodes that, due to their position in space, play a special role in broadcasting.
For instance, Figure~\ref{fig:explaining-backbone}a depicts a small network.
Considering the transmission range of each node, we can build an underlying topology as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:explaining-backbone}b.
It is easy to show that to reach full network coverage during broadcasting, it is sufficient that only the nodes in the backbone retransmit messages upon reception~\cite{Wu2001}.
The backbone depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:explaining-backbone}b is a connected dominating set (CDS).
In using this kind of backbone, nodes are partitioned in such a way that they either belong to the CDS or are directly connected to a node in the CDS.
The second property of this backbone is that all nodes in the CDS must be connected.
This kind of underlaying topology is widely used due to its efficiency and to the fact that it is relatively easy to build.
However, there are others types of underlaying topologies such as \textit{trees} and \textit{clusters} that we are not discussing in this paper~\cite{Ruiz2015}.

\paragraph{\textit{context-aware} algorithms}

Another important family is known as \textit{context-aware} algorithms.
The members of this family look at their local \textit{context} to decide if a node should retransmit a message.
For instance, nodes can consider, among other, the number of neighbors, their speed, how many neighbors have received a message~\cite{Ovalle-Martinez2006}, and the direction of the last message received.
As the contextual data may depend on the variable time, a node may choose to retransmit a message at time $t_0$ and avoid doing so at $t_0+\delta t$.
In opposition to the \textit{context-aware} family, there are also \textit{context-oblivious} algorithms that do not build a backbone.
The advantages of this strategy are that they are easy to implement and are reliable in most situations.
The downside is that they often waste resource both in terms of energy and network bandwidth.

This diversity of approaches to perform the broadcast operation is a challenge for engineers because it is hard to choose an algorithm.
One of the problems is that most algorithms are often devised to target specific operation conditions, neglecting their evaluation in other scenarios.
Moreover, despite of targeting MANETs, many studies don't consider important variables such as mobility and energy consumption.
A last factor that further complicates assessing the merit of algorithms is that frequently they have only been compared against members of their own family.
Since these limitations exists, we think it is important to compare algorithms from different families under the same conditions.
We think that, contrary to previous studies where an algorithm outperforms all others, 
the best fit for different scenarios often differ.
That is it, there is no one size fit all.
