\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate the performance and reliability of broadcast using emergent overlay using full-stack simulations with Omnet++ v5.4~\cite{omnet} and its plug-in INET (v4.1).
We compare emergent overlays with the use of baseline protocols and with two approaches to adaptive broadcast from the literature~\cite{2001:AAR:876878.879323,adaptive_group_com_iscc02}.

Our evaluation aims at answering the following research questions:
(i) Do emergent MPR overlays allow improving reliability of broadcast in zones where CF is ineffective?
(ii) Is the autonomous adaptation effective in emerging overlays without centralized control?
(iii) How do emergent overlays compare to previous approaches to adaptive broadcast in MANETs?

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Metrics.}
We evaluate broadcast protocols using the following metrics, over a series of message dissemination.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The reliability of the broadcast is measured by two metrics.
	The \emph{reach} (in \%) is the average of the proportion of nodes that receive each of the messages.
	The complete disseminations (in \%) is the fraction of messages that were received by 100\% of the nodes.
	A protocol may achieve a high reach, but never yield complete disseminations.
	%
	\item The contention on the wireless medium is measured as the number of \emph{collisions}, resulting in dropped messages and missed receptions.
	%
	\item The \emph{bandwidth} cost for nodes is measured as the number of sent and received messages.
	%
	\item The energy consumption is directly linked to the bandwidth usage and the number of collisions, due to the need for the MAC protocol to use retransmissions.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Simulation settings.}
We simulate a full MANET network stack, from the wireless medium to the application-level protocols.
The physical and data-link layers follow the IEEE 802.11g specifications.
However, we use the custom BMW~\cite{tang2001mac} MAC protocol in place of the standard virtual carrier sensing of IEEE 802.11g.

Every node uses a single transceiver with a fixed transmission range of 10m, representative e.g. of the transmission range of bluetooth and similar radio devices.
This transceiver uses the 2.4GHz frequency band, with a maximum achievable data rate of 11~Mbps.
The power consumption for each state of the transceiver are as follows: 1~mW (\emph{sleep}), 5~mW (\emph{listen}), 10~mW (\emph{receiving}) and 100~mW (\emph{transmitting}).
These is the standard model for energy consumption in INET.

\noindent
\textbf{Use case and experiment model.}
We consider a set of 600 mobile nodes.
The placement and mobility of nodes are inspired by real-life measurements of crowds at large events~\cite{naini2015opportunistic,versichele2012use}.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:setup} represents the initial position of nodes in the simulation setup.
Nodes are deployed over a 90$\times$45~m field (about the size of a soccer field).
A 40$\times$15~m area in the middle of this field represents a point of interest (POI) where nodes tend to aggregate.

At the beginning of the simulation, 300 nodes are randomly placed inside the POI and 300 nodes outside the POI.
During the simulation, nodes are mobile.
We use the \emph{truncated levy walks} model~\cite{rhee2011levy} to simulate the motion of human users.
This model implements frequent short walks as well as occasional rides to distant locations, where the direction of nodes motion remains invariable between walks.
The velocity of every walk is chosen randomly from an interval.
The interval at the POI is [0:0.5]~m/s.
Outside the POI, the interval is [1.5:2]~m/s.
These values are representative of human mobility as measured in large gatherings.
Nodes move freely within the entire communication area.
We verify upon generation of a simulation setup and mobility trace with a given seed that, at any point in time, the graph formed of all nodes and their neighbors is connected (therefore, the potential for complete coverages is theoretically 100\%).
We discard the highly infrequent seeds that do not ensure this connectedness.

% However, when a node reaches the frontier of the POI it may remain in its current region with a likelihood of 50\%. %% This is too complicated, and raises eyebrows for no reason.

Each experiment lasts 10 minutes, with one new broadcast every two second, starting from a randomly-selected source node.
Messages are of constant size 280~Bytes, similar to the length of a typical message on social media.
We run each experiment 5 times with different random seeds, and report aggregates over the five runs.

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Preconditions.}
Deployments require two inputs.
%
The first input consist in a mobility trace where nodes perform levy-walks every 0.1s.
%
At every walk we verify whether the wireless topology in the communication area is connected and keep in the mobility trace only connected topologies.
%
The second input is a list of source nodes, this selection is done randomly among those connected nodes on every wireless topology.
%
These preconditions provide a fair comparison between deployments.

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Evaluated protocols.} 
We consider our baseline protocols (counter-based CF and MPR) used in isolation, and combined under emergent overlays.
In addition, we compare to the state-of-the-art with two adaptive approaches proposed by other authors.
We implemented these two protocols in the same simulation environment, also over the BMW MAC protocol for reliable local broadcast.

Our first comparison point, \emph{adaptive counter-based controlled flooding}, or ACF, was proposed by Tseng \emph{et al.}~\cite{2001:AAR:876878.879323}.
It is an adaptive variant of counter-based CF (as described in~\S\ref{sec:background}).
Tseng~\emph{et al.} use explicit control message to collect density information around each node, but we can instead rely on the observables returned by the MAC protocol.
The setting of timers is unchanged, but the initial value of counters is set as a function of density.
We use the adaptation function suggested by Tseng~\emph{et al.} following their evaluation~\cite[Figure~6]{2001:AAR:876878.879323}, with a counter value varying from 2 to 5 for different densities (i.e., from 2 to 5 for density values 1 to 4, and then slowly down to 2 until 12+ neighbors).

Our second comparison point, \emph{S-H flooding}, was proposed by Viswanath \emph{et al.}~\cite{adaptive_group_com_iscc02}.
It requires the list of one-hop neighbors at each node, which we can also collect from the MAC protocol in place of the explicit hello messages recommended by the authors.
In addition, it requires information about the \emph{velocity} of nodes, and henceforth the use of an accelerometer or geo-location device.
It uses two types of controlled flooding protocols, \emph{scoped flooding} in dense regions and \emph{hyper flooding} in sparse regions.

\er{
Goal for this section: show experimentally that the proposed algorithms and protocols answer the research questions set forth in the introduction.
We aim at answering the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Does the use of a single protocol in an heterogeneously distributed MANET lead to sub-optimal performance?
	\item Is it beneficial to use different protocols for broadcast in a heterogeneously-distributed MANET, and does it reduce contention/collisions, costs, and improves overall reliability?
	\item What is the relative impact of heterogeneity in motion, and heterogeneity in placement, to the difference in performance and reliability observed in the complete MANET?
	\item What is the cost of the interoperability mechanism when a subset of the network decides to adapt and emerge an overlay in its region?
	\item Are the observables available locally to each node a good representation of the actual deployment environment of each node?
	\item How effective are the adaptation strategies in autonomously deciding on the emergence of overlays or the use of flooding-based broadcast?
\end{itemize}
