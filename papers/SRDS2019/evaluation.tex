\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\er{To update in intro etc.: the main drawback of MPR in dense areas is its cost, not necessarily its reliability of performance. The current intro insists too much on reliability but we should shift the focus on costs.}

We evaluate the performance and reliability of broadcast using emergent overlay using full-stack simulations with Omnet++ v5.4~\cite{omnet} and its plug-in INET (v4.1).
We compare emergent overlays with baseline protocols and with two approaches to adaptive broadcast from the literature~\cite{2001:AAR:876878.879323,adaptive_group_com_iscc02}.

Our evaluation aims at answering the following research questions:
(i) Do emergent overlays allow improving broadcast reliability in zones where CF is inefficient?
(ii) Is autonomous adaptation effective in emerging overlays without centralized control?
(iii) How do emergent overlays compare to previous approaches to adaptive broadcast?

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Simulation settings.}
We simulate a full MANET network stack, from the wireless medium to the application-level protocols.
The physical and data-link layers follow the IEEE 802.11g specifications.
However, we use the custom BMW~\cite{tang2001mac} MAC protocol in place of the standard virtual carrier sensing of IEEE 802.11g.

Every node uses a single transceiver with a fixed transmission range of 10~m, representative e.g. of the transmission range of bluetooth and similar radio devices.
This transceiver uses the 2.4~GHz frequency band, with a maximum achievable data rate of 11~Mbps.
The power consumption for each state of the transceiver are as follows: 1~mW (\emph{sleep}), 5~mW (\emph{listen}), 10~mW (\emph{receiving}) and 100~mW (\emph{transmitting}).
These is the standard model for energy consumption in INET.

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Use case and experiment model.}
We consider a set of 600 mobile nodes.
The placement and mobility of nodes are inspired by real-life measurements of crowds at large events~\cite{naini2015opportunistic,versichele2012use}.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:setup} represents the initial position of nodes in the simulation setup.
Nodes are deployed over a 90$\times$45~m field. % (about the size of a soccer field).
A 40$\times$15~m area in the middle of this field represents a point of interest (POI) where nodes tend to aggregate.

At the beginning of the simulation, 300 nodes are randomly placed inside the POI and 300 nodes outside the POI.
During the simulation, nodes are mobile.
We use the \emph{truncated levy walks}~\cite{rhee2011levy} to simulate the motion of human users.
This model implements frequent short walks as well as occasional rides to distant locations. %, where the direction of nodes motion remains invariable between walks.
The velocity of every walk is chosen randomly from an interval.
The interval at the POI is [0:0.5]~m/s.
Outside the POI, the interval is [1.5:2]~m/s.
These values are representative of human mobility as measured in large gatherings.
Nodes move freely within the entire communication area.
We verify upon generation of a mobility trace that at any point in time, the graph formed of all nodes is connected (therefore, the potential for complete coverages is theoretically 100\%).
We discard the infrequent seeds that do not ensure connectedness.

% However, when a node reaches the frontier of the POI it may remain in its current region with a likelihood of 50\%. %% This is too complicated, and raises eyebrows for no reason.

Each experiment lasts 10 minutes, with one new broadcast every two second, starting from a randomly-selected source node.
Messages are of constant size 280~Bytes, similar to the length of a typical message on social media.
We run each experiment 5 times with different random seeds, and report aggregates over the five runs.

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Evaluated protocols.} 
We consider our baseline protocols (counter-based CF and MPR) used in isolation, and combined for emergent overlays.
In addition, we compare with two state-of-the-art adaptive approaches.
We implemented these two protocols in the same simulation environment, also over the BMW MAC protocol for reliable local broadcast.

Our first comparison point is \emph{adaptive counter-based controlled flooding}, or ACF~\cite{2001:AAR:876878.879323}.
It is an adaptive variant of counter-based CF (\S\ref{sec:background}).
It uses control message to collect density information around each node.
We rely instead on the observables from the MAC protocol.
The setting of timers is unchanged, but the initial value of counters is a function of density.
We use the adaptation function suggested by the authors~\cite[Figure~6]{2001:AAR:876878.879323}.
Initial counter values vary from 2 to 5 for different densities (i.e., from 2 to 5 for density values 1 to 4, and then slowly down to 2 for 12+ neighbors).

Our second comparison point is \emph{S-H flooding}~\cite{adaptive_group_com_iscc02}.
% , was proposed by Viswanath \emph{et al.}~\cite{adaptive_group_com_iscc02}.
It uses two types of controlled flooding protocols, \emph{scoped flooding} for low-mobility nodes and \emph{hyper flooding} for high-mobility nodes.
The decision of which protocol to use is made independently by each node based on its measured \emph{velocity}, therefore requiring accelerometers.
We use 1 m/s as the switching threshold.
S-H flooding needs the list of one-hop neighbors, which is piggybacked on all forwarded messages.
As for ACF, we do not use explicit control messages but obtain these lists passively from the MAC protocol.
A node using scoped flooding compares the list of neighbors of the forwarder to its own and only retransmit is less than 85\% of the nodes overlap with its own.
Hyper flooding is a variant of timer-based controlled flooding (without the use of a counter), but where a timed retransmission is only cancelled when the duplicate is received by a node in the currently-known neighbor set of the current node.

\smallskip
\noindent
\textbf{Metrics.}
We evaluate broadcast protocols using the following metrics, over a series of message dissemination.
\begin{itemize}
	\item Reliability of the broadcast is measured by its \emph{reach} (in \%), the average proportion of nodes that receive a message.
	\item Reliability of local broadcast is measured at the MAC level by the \emph{local broadcast coverage} or \textbf{LBC} (in \%), as the average proportion of neighbors that successfully receive frames despite collisions;
	\item Overall contention on the wireless medium is measured as the number of observed \emph{collisions}, directly impacting LBC.
	\item Bandwidth cost for nodes is measured as the number of sent and received messages.
	\item Energy consumption is directly linked to the bandwidth usage and the number of collisions, due to the need for the MAC protocol to use retransmissions.
\end{itemize}

We start by evaluating the performance and reliability of the base protocols.
Table~\ref{eval:baseline:coverage-collisions} reports reliability metrics for two variants of CF with initial counter values of 3 and 4, and two variants of MPR with period $\Delta$ of 20 and 30 seconds.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:baseline_performance} details the network traffic at the application level (i.e. forwarded messages, without control traffic).
We distinguish between outgoing messages (i.e. forwarded messages) and incoming messages (i.e. received messages).
The difference between the former and the latter stands for duplicates.
We present all metrics for the entire system, and the breakdown outside and inside the central point of interest (POI).

We can observe that CF with an initial counter value of 3 achieves a good reach in the entire system, but only through a high number of duplicates.
This results in low LBC and a high number of collisions.
This is particularly true at the POI where the LBC goes down to 35.09\%, and where the number of collisions is significantly higher than outside of the POI for the same number of nodes.
The use of a higher value of the counter ($\text{c}=4$) improves reach but also results in a major inefficiency in particular at the POI.

MPR is able to reach a 100\% reach (with $\Delta=20s$) for nodes at the POI, with a significantly better LBC than CF, and less collisions.
The reliability out of the POI is good but not as high as with CF.
We note that MPR with control messages periods $\Delta$ lower than 20 seconds yield a significant number of collisions and suffer a loss in reliability.

The evaluation of individual protocols confirms that CF is inefficient in dense zones, where MPR is a better choice.
We choose to use MPR with $\Delta=30s$ for emergent overlays, and CF with $\text{c}=3$ otherwise.
