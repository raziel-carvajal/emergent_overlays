We start by detailing our and communication system model.
We present a MAC protocol for reliable \emph{local} broadcast.
We finally introduce the two broadcast protocols, one based on controlled flooding, the other based on an overlay, that we combine to allow emergent overlays.

\subsection{System and communication models}
\label{sec:background:model}

An arbitrary number of nodes $n_{i},n_{j},\dots$ are mobile in a 2-dimensional space.
They communicate using wireless transceivers that can emit network frames using \emph{local broadcast}.
We consider the fixed-range model: Local broadcasts can be received by every node within a transmission range Tx.
Figure~\ref{fig:manet-comm-model} presents the coverage areas for nodes $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$.
Node $n_{3}$ is in range of both and may receive frames from either $n_{1}$ or $n_{2}$ when active in listening mode (i.e., not sending itself).
Sending and receiving frames consumes energy from the node's limited power supply.
We do not consider the availability of geolocation information (GPS) on the devices.

The main obstacle to reliable broadcast in MANETs is the presence of \emph{collisions}.
A collision may happen when two nodes have overlapping coverage areas.
If nodes $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ send frames at the same time then it is impossible for $n_{3}$ to successfully receive either of the frames.
The same applies to a node such as $n_4$ that is currently sending and does not receive locally broadcast frames from other nodes in range.
Detecting collisions as they happen is not possible.
This phenomenon is known as the hidden terminal problem~\cite{hidden-terminal-problem}.

\subsection{MAC protocol for local broadcast}
\label{sec:background:mac}

A MAC (Medium Access Control) protocol arbitrates the use of the communication medium to minimize collisions.
It detects missed receptions and implement frame retransmissions when necessary.
% There is a tradeoff between the number and cost of such retransmissions, and their impact on collisions: Too many retransmissions may make things worse and create a local broadcast storm~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem}.
% Another difficulty is due to mobility: Nodes are not aware of the identities of other nodes within their coverage areas.

The IEEE 802.11 coordination function is a classical MAC protocol for reliable point-to-point communication, i.e. where the single destination for the message is known.
It relies on a collision avoidance mechanism known as \emph{virtual carrier sensing}~\cite{ieee1997wireless}.
It uses a handshake protocol.
The source locally broadcasts a small Request To Send (RTS) frame.
The destination node replies with a Clear To Send (CTS) frame and switches to listening mode for a pre-defined time.
The reception of the full packet (series of frames) is then acknowledged by the destination.
The sending can be repeated up to three times in case of missed/error-bearing acknowledgments.

Our MAC protocol targets \emph{efficient local broadcast} instead of point-to-point communication.
It was developed independently but follows similar principles as the broadcast medium window protocol~\cite{tang2001mac}.
The source wishes to send the same message to all the nodes that are in its coverage area.
We modify the handshake to enable the discovery of these nodes.
All nodes reply with a CTS frame containing their identity, following the RTS of the sender.
Physical carrier sensing~\cite{ieee1997wireless} allows minimizing the risk of collisions between CTS replies:
Nodes wait for an initial random duration and probe the wireless medium before sending; If it is currently used, they back off and retry after an (exponentially increasing) duration.
This enables the sender to obtain a snapshot of its local neighborhood.
The sender collects acknowledgments from all nodes (also using physical carrier sensing to avoid collisions) and may retry locally broadcasting a frame up to three times, as in the original protocol.
Our rationale is that beyond three failures for a node due to collisions indicate a highly contended medium, and using more re-sending will likely result in a local broadcast storm~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem} and make things even worse.

\subsection{(Global) Broadcast protocols}
\label{sec:background:broadcast}

The goal of global broadcast (hereafter referred simply as broadcast) is to reliably send a message to all nodes in the network, despite mobility and collisions.
The source can be any node in the network.
Nodes \emph{may} forward a message using local broadcast upon its first reception.
We denote such nodes as \emph{relay} nodes.
Subsequent receptions are \emph{duplicates} that are simply ignored.
The target is to reduce the number of necessary relay nodes while ensuring a high (or full) \emph{coverage}, measured as the number of nodes that receive the message during the broadcast.
We note that incomplete broadcasts may be completed using a palliative mechanism such as periodic anti-entropy~\cite{leme2017analyzing}.

Broadcast protocols can be classified in two families depending on how they select relay nodes~\cite{survey-ruiz}.
In controlled flooding, nodes decide autonomously whether to be a relay, for each received message.
In overlay-based approaches, the selection of relays is the result of an overlay creation process that requires the prior exchange of specific control messages.
We present in the following the two such protocols that we use in the rest of this paper.

\label{sec:background:broadcast:flooding}

Pure flooding is the simplest broadcast protocol where every node acts as a relay for every message.
Relaying may happen after a random waiting time to reduce the risk of collisions~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem}.
Some approaches even decide on a time value that is dependent on an estimation of the collision risk around the node, but require geographical (GPS) coordinates to compute this estimation~\cite{abba}.

We use a counter-based controlled flooding~\cite{2001:AAR:876878.879323} that is both simple and efficient in a variety of practical situations, as comparative studies have shown~\cite{DensityMobDrivenEval,Williams2002}.
Upon first reception of a message, a node sets up a timer $t$ and a counter $c=4$.
The duration of $t$ is chosen randomly in the range $[10^{-6}, 10^{-4})$ seconds.
Every reception of a duplicate decreases $c$ by one.
Upon expiration of $t$, the node only acts as a relay if $c>0$.
An illustration of the process is given by Figure~\ref{fig:background:flooding}.

\subsubsection{Multipoint Relay (MPR)}
\label{sec:background:broadcast:overlay}

Overlay-based approaches pre-compute which nodes should act as relays.
This requires exchange of information between nodes outside of actual broadcast sessions.
The goal is to form a connected dominating set, where the coverage area of all relays encompasses all nodes in the network, is itself a connected component, and ideally contains the smallest possible number of relays.
The overlay can be \emph{explicit} when nodes are aware of their status of relay nodes~\cite{stojmenovic-cds,Nikolov2015}.
It may also be implicit, when nodes are informed they should act as a relay from the source or previous relay in the dissemination path.
We adopt a protocol of the latter type, MPR~\cite{mpr} (\emph{Multipoint relaying}).

MPR collects at each node its set of one-hop neighbors, i.e. those in its coverage area, as well as two-hops neighbors.
Nodes periodically locally broadcast their current set of one-hop neighbors and passively collect or update the information received from their neighbors.
Information about a neighbor is reset after a few missed announcements, to account for nodes leaving the coverage area.
The frequency of announcements depends on the dynamism of the local network.
Too frequent announcements bear the risk of extra collisions and energy costs, while infrequent ones may lead to overlays that are no longer connected or dominating.

Each node in MPR selects locally amongst its one-hop neighbors which should act as relays in future disseminations.
This decision is local, avoiding the extra communication round required by the creation of an explicit overlay.
The selection is detailed by Algorithm~\ref{algorithm:mpr}.
All two-hop neighbors are initially considered uncovered by future dissemination, and the list of relays starts empty (lines 6-7).
The selection is in two phase.
In a first phase, isolated two-hop neighbors are detected and the one-hop neighbor connecting to these is selected as a relay (the two-hop neighborhood of that relay contains only the local node $n_i$ and this two-hop neighbor $u$, line 9).
In a second phase, nodes are considered in decreasing order of their number of neighbors, and added to the relay set until all two-hop neighbors are covered.
The interest of the first phase is to avoid going down the list until the lowly-connected nodes are considered, after all other nodes have been selected as relays.
MPR is a simple heuristic that may not guarantee the optimal selection of relays, but it has the advantage of requiring little computation and no GPS coordinates, and works well in practice.
