We start by detailing our system and communication models.
We follow-up with the description of a MAC protocol supporting reliable \emph{local} broadcast.
We finally introduce the two (global) broadcast protocols, one based on controlled flooding, the other using an overlay, that we will later combine.

\subsection{System and communication models}
\label{sec:background:model}

A set $N$ of nodes $n_{i},n_{j},\dots$ are mobile in a 2-dimensional space.
We do not consider the availability of geolocation information (GPS) on the devices.
Nodes communicate using wireless transceivers that can emit network frames using \emph{local broadcast} and receive frames when active in listening mode.
Nodes cannot send and receive simultaneously.
We consider the fixed-range model: Local broadcasts can be received by every node within a transmission range Tx.
Figure~\ref{fig:manet-comm-model} presents the coverage areas for nodes $n_{1\dots4}$.
Node $n_{3}$ is in range of both $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ and may receive frames from either node.
Sending and receiving frames consumes energy from the node's limited power supply.

The main obstacle to reliable broadcast in MANETs is the presence of \emph{collisions}.
A collision may happen when two nodes have overlapping coverage areas.
If nodes $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ send frames at the same time then it is impossible for $n_{3}$ to successfully receive both of the frames, or even any of them.
The same applies to a node such as $n_4$ that is in sending mode and cannot receive locally broadcast frames from other nodes in range.
Detecting collisions as they happen is not possible~\cite{ieee1997wireless}.

\subsection{MAC protocol for local broadcast}
\label{sec:background:mac}

A MAC (\emph{Medium Access Control}) protocol arbitrates the use of the wireless medium to minimize collisions and their impact on communication reliability.
It detects missed receptions and implement frame retransmissions when necessary.
% There is a tradeoff between the number and cost of such retransmissions, and their impact on collisions: Too many retransmissions may make things worse and create a local broadcast storm~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem}.
% Another difficulty is due to mobility: Nodes are not aware of the identities of other nodes within their coverage areas.

The IEEE 802.11 coordination function is a classical MAC protocol for reliable point-to-point communication, i.e. where the single destination for the frame is known.
It relies on a collision avoidance mechanism known as \emph{virtual carrier sensing}~\cite{ieee1997wireless}.
It uses a handshake protocol.
The source locally broadcasts a small Request To Send (RTS) control frame.
The destination node replies with a Clear To Send (CTS) control frame and switches to listening mode for a predefined time.
The reception of the frame is then acknowledged by the destination.
The sending can be repeated up to three times in case of missed or error-bearing acknowledgments.

Our MAC protocol targets \emph{efficient local broadcast} instead of point-to-point communication.
It was developed independently but follows similar principles as the broadcast medium window protocol~\cite{tang2001mac}.
It is illustrated by Figure~\ref{fig:mac_protocol}.
The source wishes to send the same frame to all the nodes that are in its coverage area.
We modify the handshake to enable the \emph{discovery} of these nodes.
All nodes reply with a CTS containing their identity, following the RTS of the sender.
Physical carrier sensing~\cite{ieee1997wireless} allows minimizing the risk of collisions between CTS replies:
Nodes wait for an initial random duration and probe the wireless medium before sending; If it is currently used, they back off and retry after an (exponentially increasing) duration.
This enables the sender to obtain a snapshot of its local neighborhood.
The sender collects acknowledgments from all nodes (also using physical carrier sensing to avoid collisions) and may retry locally broadcasting a frame up to three times, as in the original protocol.
Our rationale is that more than three failures for a local broadcast due to collisions indicate a highly contended medium, and using more re-sending will likely result in a local broadcast storm~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem} and further deteriorate the situation.

\subsection{(Global) Broadcast protocols}
\label{sec:background:broadcast}

The goal of \emph{global} broadcast (hereafter referred simply as broadcast) is to reliably send a message to all nodes in the network, despite mobility and collisions.
The source can be any node in the network, and the message may require multiple frames.\footnote{A session protocol assists in splitting a message in multiple frames if necessary and calling the MAC protocol.
We do not detail this protocol as it is straightforward to design and implement.}
Nodes \emph{may} forward a message using local broadcast upon its first reception.
We denote such nodes as \emph{relay} nodes.
Subsequent receptions are \emph{duplicates} that are simply ignored.
The target is to reduce the number of necessary relay nodes while ensuring a high (or full) \emph{coverage}, measured as the number of nodes that receive the message during the broadcast.
We note that incomplete broadcasts may be completed using a palliative mechanism such as periodic anti-entropy~\cite{leme2017analyzing}.

Broadcast protocols can be classified in two families depending on how they select relay nodes~\cite{survey-ruiz}.
In controlled flooding, nodes decide autonomously whether to be a relay, for each received message.
In overlay-based approaches, the selection of relays is the result of an overlay creation process that requires the prior exchange of control messages.
We present in the following two such protocols.

\subsubsection{Counter-based controlled flooding}
\label{sec:background:broadcast:flooding}

Uncontrolled (``pure'') flooding is the simplest broadcast protocol where every node acts as a relay for every message.
It generates a potentially large number of duplicates, and many concurrent retransmissions in particular in dense areas.
This generates a high number of collisions and associated retransmissions at the MAC level.
The risk of collision may be reduced by waiting before retransmitting, either for a random amount of time~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem} or, when GPS coordinates are available, for a time that depends on a local density estimation~\cite{abba} but these mitigations do not reduce the number of duplicates.

Controlled flooding enables nodes to decide \emph{not to} retransmit, when they estimate that their neighborhood has been sufficient covered.
We use the classical counter-based approach~\cite{BroadcastStormProblem} that is efficient in a variety of practical situations, as comparative studies have shown~\cite{DensityMobDrivenEval,Williams2002}.
Upon first reception of a message, a node sets up a timer $t$ and a counter $c$.
Every reception of a duplicate decreases $c$ by one.
Upon expiration of $t$, the node only acts as a relay if $c>0$.

Controlled flooding is intuitively good for regions with high mobility, as the decision of whether or not to be a relay is made by each node independently based on its observation of the local neighborhood at dissemination time.
Setting the value of the timer $t$ and of the counter $c$ is however difficult, as optimal values depend on node density.
For sparse areas, too low a value of $c$ will lead to incomplete coverage.
In dense areas, too high a value of $c$ will lead to a high number of collisions and duplicates.
We choose to favor coverage in sparse areas and use $t \in [10^{-6}, 10^{-4})$ seconds and $c=4$.
An illustration of counter-based controlled flooding is given by Figure~\ref{fig:background:flooding} using $c=2$.

\subsubsection{Multipoint Relay (MPR)}
\label{sec:background:broadcast:overlay}

Overlay-based approaches pre-compute which nodes should act as relays.
This requires exchange of information between nodes outside of actual broadcast sessions.
The goal is to form a \emph{connected dominating set} overlay
The combined coverage areas of all relays must contain all nodes in the network.
The overlay should be connected but at the same time contain as little relays as possible.
It can be \emph{explicit} when nodes are aware of their status of relay nodes~\cite{stojmenovic-cds,Nikolov2015}.
It may also be implicit, when nodes are informed they should act as a relay from the source or previous relay in the dissemination process.
We adopt a protocol of the latter type, MPR~\cite{mpr,mpr-improvement} (\emph{Multipoint Relay}).

The principle of MPR is to let each node decide which of its one-hop (direct) neighbors should be instructed to act as relays during a dissemination in which it participates as a relay or source itself.
It requires the discovery of all one-hop and two-hop neighbors using periodic control messages.
Nodes periodically announce themselves using local broadcast and include their current neighborhood, as obtained from the last exchanges at the MAC level.
Information about a neighbor is reset after a few missed announcements, to account for nodes leaving the coverage area.
The ideal frequency of announcements depends on the network dynamics.
Too frequent announcements bear the risk of extra collisions and energy costs, while infrequent ones may lead to overlays that are no longer connected or dominating.

The selection of relays is detailed by Algorithm~\ref{algorithm:mpr} and illustrated by Figure~\ref{fig:background:mpr}.
The goal is to ensure that all two-hop neighbors are \emph{covered} by at least one relay.
The selection is in two phases.
The first phase (lines 8--11) selects relays that are \emph{necessary}, i.e. one-hop neighbors that are the only connection to ``isolated'' two-hop neighbors.
The second phase (lines 12--16) selects relays that are \emph{sufficient} to complete the coverage.
The heuristic is add as relay nodes that cover the largest number of two-hop neighbors, until all nodes are covered.
As the set of uncovered nodes is the union of all two-hop neighbors (line 6), the \texttt{while} loop on lines 12--15 always terminate.
MPR is a local heuristic that may not guarantee the optimal selection of relays, but it has the advantage of requiring little computation and no GPS coordinates.
It also does not require specific synchronization between nodes to agree on an explicit overlay (e.g. to inform nodes who must act as relays), reducing the cost of its maintenance.

An overlay-based broadcast is expected to be useful in scenario where nodes density is high, greatly reducing the number of relays without requiring a difficult-to-set counter or time value.
It is however necessary that the nodes positions be sufficiently stable, or the frequency of control messages will need to be increased to a point where they will create collisions and potentially bear higher costs than for the dissemination themselves.
