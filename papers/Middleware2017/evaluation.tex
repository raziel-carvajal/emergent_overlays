\subsection{Simulation Environment}
\labsection{simulation-env}

Our experiments are inspired by the scenario of a crowd of users carrying portable communication devices (e.g., smartphones) in an area with no infrastructure connectivity.
%
In this area, there are several points of interest where people tend to stay.
%
As a result, the density of devices in the points of interest is high, and the mobility is low.
%
Meanwhile, fewer nodes constantly move in the area between point of interests. 
%
The dissemination of information from one individual to all the others is critical, and uses ad-hoc connections between devices.
%
Our evaluation of broadcast algorithms for MANETs is mainly driven by two factors: \emph{network density} and \emph{nodes mobility}.
%
We describe in the following the parameters used in our experiments.


\paragraph{Simulator.}
We rely on OMNET++4.6/INET3.3, a simulation framework for the evaluation of networking algorithms~\cite{Varga:2008:OOS:1416222.1416290}.
%
This tool provides a full implementation of the IEEE 802.11g standard.
%
In addition, it contains models for the mobility of nodes and for energy consumption.


\paragraph{Energy Model.}
%
The power consumed by a node mainly depends on the wireless communication chip it uses.
%
We selected the Broadcom BCM4329~\cite{BCM4329}, a commercial chip commonly found in modern mobile devices and which supports Wi-Fi 802.11g on the 2.4 GHz frequency band. As described in \reftab{wifi-chip}, the energy consumed by the chip varies from only 648 nW in sleep mode to up to 1.206 W when the transmitter is active.

\paragraph{Communication Scenario.}
Simulated participants use their devices to broadcast messages without external coordination and may attempt to do so at nearly the same time.
%
We set our experiments to perform 200 broadcast sessions with a frequency between broadcasts of $0.1$s.
%
During a broadcast session, a randomly selected node acts as a source and broadcasts a randomly generated message.
%
Each of the 200 messages is 32~bytes long and contains a unique identifier.
%
Moreover, we assume normal wireless communications range and no environmental interference.
%
The only reason to lost packets in the medium is due to collisions, which are handle by CSMA/CA protocol.

\paragraph{Simulation time.}
As mentioned in~\refsection{mechanism}, some algorithms need to build a topology.
%
To guarantee that the first broadcast message is properly handled by all protocols, we empirically set an initial warm-up period of 2s, allowing CDS-based algorithms to perform this initial construction.
%
Similarly, we  wait 2s after the last broadcast session.
%
The overall simulation time is therefore of 24 seconds.


\paragraph{Mobility Model.}
We use a mobility model with points of interest (PoI) proposed by Piorkowski et al.~\cite{mobility_density_model}.
%
In this model, all nodes move in short steps that are defined using two Gaussian distributions.
%
To do so, nodes are classified in two groups: nodes in the vecinity of a PoI use a distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_1^{2})$, and nodes in between PoIs use $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_2^{2})$ where $\sigma_1 <  \sigma_2$.
%
As a consequence, nodes in the vecinity of a PoI move slower than nodes in between PoIs.
%
They also stay most of the time in a PoI making the density in PoI  higher than elsewhere.

To generate the traces, nodes are initially uniformly distributed on a map of 250 x 250 m.
%
During each step of the simulation, nodes move according to the group in which they belong to.   
%
\reftab{mobility} summarizes the configuration parameters we used.

%
Although we check that the initial network is connected, we cannot guarantee that all nodes are reachable for the whole duration of the experiment.
%
We argue that it does not affect the experiments because all protocols are evaluated under the same conditions.


\paragraph{Algorithm-specific parameters.}
The behavior of each protocol depends on its parameters, for which we favor values previously reported in the literature.
%
For instance, we use 1.5s as the \emph{hello time} for \emph{MPR}.
%
In the case of \emph{ABBA}, we use 60 milleseconds as the maximum waiting time.
%

For adaptive approaches, we only allow switching between two protocols.
%
At the beginning of the simulation, each node is executing \textit{simple flooding}.
%
Afterwards, a node is free to change its protocol based on some policy.
%
However, it can only change back and forth between \textit{simple flooding} and \textit{MPR}.
%
We are selecting these two algorithms based on previous studies that show their benefit in the scenarios we are considering~\cite{DensityMobDrivenEval}.  
%

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

To properly compare our approach against previous solutions, we present the results of our experiments in terms of a set of well-established metrics:


\paragraph{Coverage.} 
The percentage of nodes that successfully receive a broadcast message at least once.
%
Since we are performing several broadcast sessions, we are interested in the evolution of this metric over time.

%
\paragraph{Saved Rebroadcasts (SRE).} 
The number of forwarding avoided by using a given algorithm  compared to \emph{simple flooding}.
%
To compute the SRE of one algorithm $A$ in a network of $N$ nodes, we measure the number $R_m$ of retransmissions made by $A$ to disseminate a message $m$. The $ \textit{SRE}_m$ for message $m$ is then $N - R_m$.
%
Since in our experiments we are disseminating many messages, we compute the mean \emph{SRE} and use it as metric for our comparison study.
%
This metric is useful to assess the performance of algorithms in terms of both network and energy usage.

%\noindent $\triangleright$
%\textbf{Duplicate messages} --
%the number of times one broadcast message is received again after its first reception.
%


\paragraph{Broadcast delay time.} 
The time to disseminate a message with a coverage of 100\%.
%
The broadcast time helps to understand which algorithm to use with an application.
%
For instance, some applications tolerate high-latencies while others need fast responses.


% 
\paragraph{Energy consumption.}
The energy consumed by nodes for the broadcast of all messages and, when applicable, for the construction and maintenance of the overlay.

\subsection{Comparison with base protocols}
\labsection{comparison-base-protocols}


\reffigure{coverage} shows the coverage obtained when different algorithms are used to disseminate messages in MANET.
%
In particular, it depicts the results for previous approaches such as \textit{ABBA}, \textit{MPR}, \textit{simple flooding} and our adaptive solution.
%
The first thing to notice is that this network is often disconnected, for that reason the coverage is less than 100\% for many broadcast sessions.
%
The second interesting point is that our adaptive solutions outperform both \textit{ABBA} and \textit{MPR}.
%
In fact, the adaptive approach often follows the same trend as \textit{flooding}.
%
However, this pattern does not occur during two ranges of broadcast sessions: before session 50, and short after session 100.
%
In both cases, this happens because some nodes have switched from \textit{flooding} to \textit{MPR}, but \textit{MPR} is still warming up and the node is not acting a relay when it should be doing so.
%
Finally, the adaptation policy has little effect in terms of coverage.
Indeed, although in a few broadcast session the \textit{local} policy behaves better than \textit{SWSP}, the difference is negligible.

Using the results shown in~\refsection{comparison-base-protocols}, we can answer research questions \textit{RQ2} and \textit{RQ3}.

%
Concretely, using our adaptive approaches instead of previous solutions can be beneficial in some scenarios with PoIs.
%
This answer the research question \textit{RQ2}.
%
In particular, \textit{flooding}, the only protocol with a slightly better coverage than our solutions, consumes more energy.
%
On the other hand, protocols such as \textit{MPR} that has low power consumption, fail in delivering a good coverage.
%
Likewise, \textit{ABBA} behaves quite poorly in terms of coverage; also in terms of energy usage if the GPS is considered.
%

Unfortunately, we have not found a conclusive answer to research question \textit{RQ3}.
%
That is, we cannot observe significant differences in the two adaptation policies we compare.
%
For instance, \textit{SWSP} has a slight advantage in terms of energy consumption, but the difference is negligible.
%
Meanwhile, the \textit{Local} adaptation policy is marginally better in terms of \textit{broadcast delay time}.
%
In conclusion, to the extent of our experiments, we are unable to identify differences.
%
More extensive experiments are required, probably including other policies like the one discussed in~\refalg{alg:policies:two_phase_collective_policy}.


%
It is worth noticing that the validity of these results is limited to the scenarios described in~\refsection{simulation-env}.
%
That is, scenarios where there are several PoIs in an area, people tend to stay in the PoIs, and them a few of them move between PoIs.
