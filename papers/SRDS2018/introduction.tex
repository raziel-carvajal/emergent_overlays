\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Mobile Ad-hoc Networks (MANETs) are wireless networks that do not relay on fixed infrastructures, such as access points, antennas or routers.
%
The participants or nodes in these networks operate on battery powered devices, nodes move following patterns hard to predict and they communicate with each other in a peer-to-peer fashion.
%
As a result of these operating conditions, we obtain a dynamic mobile system.
%
Real-life scenarios are dynamic systems as well, and MANETs characterize some of their changing conditions.
%
For instance, restricted zones that are used for military exercises.
%
Figure~\ref{fig:manet-example} depicts another scenario, a city that has deployed its mitigation program to deal with a natural catastrophe.
%
Cellular networks or other communication systems are not reliable in this scenario, and at the same time, people need to be informed of latest events.
%
We notice an heterogeneous amount of people (represented with dots) spread over every zone in the figure.
%
Shelters or canteens usually gather more people than others zones such as information desks or corridors to allow transit between point of interests.
%
Another characteristic is mobility, civilians receiving instructions may remain more static from paramedics or police officers that are in constant movement.
%
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[scale=0.32]{figures/protest.png}
	\caption{Examples of real-life scenarios where MANETs may be deployed.}
	\label{fig:manet-example}
\end{figure}
%
A messaging service deployed on the top of a MANET where people use their mobile phones (or any other wireless device) for relying information, may improve the efficiency of the mitigation program shown in figure~\ref{fig:manet-example}.
%
On the contrarily, reliable communication in such scenario is a challenging task.

Broadcast is a fundamental operation in MANETs that consist in delivering a message from a source node to every other node in the network.
%
Data dissemination in crowds, peer-to-peer mobile games or routing are examples of services that relay on this operation~\cite{overview_adhoc_networks}.
%
A vast amount of broadcast protocols have been proposed to figure out a trade-off between reachability and nodes resources (mainly energy consumption).
%
A survey by Ruiz and Bouvry~\cite{SurveyRuiz} classifies protocols in two main families.
%
Protocols of the first group rely on overlays where nodes either forward incoming messages or act as receivers. \er{You should already emphasize that the overlay is built outside of the actual dissemination, while the flooding decides during the dissemination. This is the main difference that concerns us.}
%
Nodes build and maintain an overlay via the periodic exchange of control packets that announce nodes existence. \er{not sure we need to detail how the overlay is built here}
%
The second group of broadcast protocols do not build overlays, instead, nodes forward messages until predefined thresholds reach a maximum value (e.x. number of receives copies) or the forwarding decision is based on a stochastic approach. \er{you are too specific about the implementations of flooding. It should be enough to write that the nodes make the decision to forward or not based on local information available at the time the message is received.}
%
Reliable broadcast remains a challenging task, not only for deciding which is the protocol that shows better performance but also due to the operating conditions in MANETs.
%
Moreover, previous works on mobility models for wireless networks~\cite{bai2004mobility, ChaintreauA2006}, evaluation studies on broadcast strategies~\cite{DensityMobDrivenEval, graph-mob-model_Sholz2005} or micro-benchmarks on highly dynamic networks~\cite{adaptive_group_com_iscc02, ObraczkaK_2001} have shown that the performance of protocols for MANETs is influenced by operating conditions in the network, where mobility and density are key factors.

We propose an autonomous adaptation protocol for MANETs that allow nodes to automatically emerge overlays where they are beneficial for broadcast cost and performance, and dynamically adapt to changing mobility and density conditions.
%
Nodes follow one main switching criterion that is triggered when the local perspective of nodes reaches predefined thresholds.
%
That is, overlays emerge in highly dense areas where nodes move slowly whereas controlled flooding techniques are deployed in dynamic areas with low density.
%
Dissemination overlays require nodes to keep track of their closest neighbors.
%
Dense areas with low velocity avoid abrupt changes in nodes surroundings, therefore, we find an opportunity for having more stable overlays that are easy to maintain.
%
Deploying controlled flooding techniques in more dynamic areas relies on previous studies on broadcast protocols~\cite{adaptive_group_com_iscc02} that have shown better performance. \raziel{add references to flooding approaches in sparse with low velocity of nodes}
%
Previous works on adaptive approaches for message dissemination in MANETs have shown that there is no one-fit-all solution~\cite{adaptive_group_com_iscc02}, we agree with this notion.\raziel{add references of adaptive approaches}.
%
Our adaptive approach, on the other hand, fills the gap in providing generalized solutions that integrate different broadcast techniques and deal with the interoperability issues of having multiple protocols running concurrently in a deployed MANET.
%
In concrete, our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item A novel self-adaptive approach to broadcast in MANETs where nodes follows a main switching criterion, that is, the deployment of dissemination overlays in densely-populated areas whereas controlled flooding techniques are used in sparsely-populated areas.
	\item A list of adaptive policies mainly based on nodes velocity and density to trigger our switching criterion. Such list may be easily extended with other information available in nodes.
	\item To our knowledge, there is no previous work on broadcast protocols for MANETs that uses the local degree of nodes as a measure of density. We use the local clustering coefficient and a recently propose variation of it called closure coefficient~\cite{closure_coef} for the design of adaptation policies.
	\item Our adaptive approach is available to the research community in form of a framework, which uses Docker containers to help in the reproducibility of results~\cite{emergent_overlays_repo}.
\end{itemize}
